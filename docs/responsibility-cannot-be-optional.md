# Responsibility Cannot Be Optional

**An open question to the companies building AI, the governments allowing it into society, the
enterprises adopting it, and every human using it.**

With more capability comes more responsibility.

That responsibility cannot be optional when the capability affects people who never chose to
participate. General-purpose AI learned from human work at internet scale. It can now reproduce
language, image, voice and skilled tasks at world scale. Yet the people whose work made that
capability possible often cannot tell whether their work was used, under what authority, or who
benefited. The people affected by an AI output may have no relationship with the company that built
the model or the human who directed it.

AI can improve people's lives. That possibility does not excuse adopting it without accounting for
the responsibility it moves, weakens or leaves behind.

The central finding is not that institutions have never thought about responsibility. They have.
The failure is that what they learn does not reliably control what they release next. Capability
continues by default; responsibility must repeatedly win an internal, political or legal argument.

---

## The questions that need direct answers

### About the data

- What works, images, voices, code, conversations and personal data trained the model?
- Can one person discover whether their work or identity was included?
- What made each use permissible: ownership, a licence, consent, public domain or a legal exception?
- Where permission was not obtained, who decided that it was unnecessary?
- When people use the product, what is collected, how long is it kept, who may review it, and does it
  train a future model?

### About the value

- Who was paid for the material that created the capability?
- Where a platform or publisher licensed a collection, how did that value reach the people who made
  the underlying work?
- Why is there no general mechanism for attribution or continuing compensation when a model earns
  from the work of millions?

### About release

- What was tested before the model reached the public?
- Which people and communities were represented in that test?
- What known failures remained at release?
- Who independent of the developer had the evidence and authority to stop or limit the release?
- Why did a "research preview" become available worldwide instead of moving through bounded trials
  whose reach increased only when the observed outcome supported it?

### About harm

- Who can stop a false likeness, unsafe action or rights violation before it happens?
- Who monitors what the agent actually does, not only what it was instructed to do?
- Who preserves evidence, tells the affected person, removes the harm and pays for it?
- When a human role is removed, who inherits its judgement and accountability?

These are not questions against AI. They are the questions that let society use AI without making
other people carry costs they never accepted.

## What the evidence establishes

### They know, but the learning does not control power

Leading AI companies publish detailed frontier-risk frameworks. Governments have named safety,
accountability, governance and redress, and the EU has enacted binding duties for defined risks.
So the persistent failure cannot honestly be reduced to "they do not know how."

The evidence points to a harder structure. Capability has common benchmarks, rapid investment and
immediate rewards. Frontier safety governance is often company-run. At OpenAI, its internal Safety
Advisory Group recommends action while leadership makes the final release decision. Its framework
also says a competitor's high-risk release can lead it to adjust requirements, but only after stated
risk checks, public acknowledgement and a continuing-protection commitment. Governments are
simultaneously regulators, investors, purchasers, promoters and geopolitical competitors.
Responsibility is divided across providers, deployers, platforms, users and jurisdictions until no
actor owns the whole Outcome.

Institutions do learn: policies are revised and some failures are corrected. But an incident does
not automatically trigger independent investigation, a temporary limit, a verified correction and
a common rule change. That is Outcome without Growth.

[Read why repeated incidents still do not become binding accountability.](ai-responsibility/07-why-incidents-do-not-become-accountability.md)

### The risks were not unforeseen

Researchers and governments named responsibility, privacy, human control, shared prosperity,
documented training data and pre-development risk assessment years before ChatGPT. The record runs
from the 2017 Asilomar Principles, through the 2019 OECD Principles and 2021 UNESCO Recommendation,
to the 2021 *Stochastic Parrots* paper and the 2023 request for a pause.

What was missing was not language. It was a boundary backed by authority over release.

[Read the warning record and its limits.](ai-responsibility/01-it-was-not-unforeseen.md)

### A public research preview was not a controlled trial

Medicine has an external gate: preclinical evidence, permission before human trials, bounded phases,
review before marketing and monitoring afterwards. That structure exists because earlier law
allowed an untested medicine to kill more than 100 people.

ChatGPT launched free to the public as a research preview, with known limitations, and reached an
estimated 100 million monthly users in two months. Public use supplied evidence to the developer,
but the public was not protected by a medicine-like protocol or approval decision.

The EU now has meaningful pre-market requirements for defined high-risk AI, but it is not a
universal approval gate for general-purpose models and it arrived after mass adoption.

[Read the trial comparison and a staged-release proposal.](ai-responsibility/02-the-missing-trial-gate.md)

### Internet scale hid the individual source

The record supports multi-trillion-token web corpora and billions of image-text pairs. It does not
support the literal claim that a model contains "the entire world's data." The real problem does not
need that exaggeration.

Major model documents often disclose categories such as "publicly available online data" or "web
documents" rather than a work-level source list. GPT-4's technical report explicitly withheld
dataset-construction detail. Current consumer services also differ in whether conversations may be
used for improvement by default or only after a choice.

Licensing and contributor payment are possible: some publishers and platforms have licences, and
Shutterstock says its contributor fund has paid artists and provides ongoing royalties. Those
arrangements are selected, contractual islands. They are not a general right to attribution,
permission or payment for everyone whose work contributed to model capability.

[Read the data, consent and compensation evidence.](ai-responsibility/03-data-consent-and-compensation.md)

### Law does not cover the whole moral injury

Copyright normally protects the photographer, not automatically the person shown. US likeness law
is fragmented. The TAKE IT DOWN Act covers non-consensual intimate imagery, not every false video.
The broader NO FAKES Act had been reported by a Senate committee but was not law on 6 September 2026.
The EU requires deepfake disclosure, but a label does not establish consent.

A fake video of a friend can violate their dignity and safety even where no clear legal claim reaches
the facts. **The absence of a rule is not permission.**

[Read the likeness, privacy and moral-gap analysis.](ai-responsibility/04-likeness-privacy-and-the-moral-gap.md)

### A model rule is not an enforced boundary

Providers already publish prohibited-use rules and train models to refuse harmful requests. Their
own reports also acknowledge jailbreaks and classification errors. Open-weight safeguards can be
modified or removed after release; closed services retain stronger control over access but cannot
infer a consent agreement or legal authority they have never seen.

The model can warn and refuse. The provider controls the model, product and release. The enterprise
controls permissions and deployment. The human controls whether to override or route around the
boundary. Government controls what none of them may waive.

[Read what each layer can and cannot enforce.](ai-responsibility/05-why-model-rules-are-not-enforcement.md)

### Livelihood effects are real, uneven and not fully measured

The evidence does not show economy-wide mass replacement. It does show reduced work and earnings in
some exposed freelance occupations, 20-50% demand declines in selected writing and translation
tasks, and a growing relative employment gap for young workers in exposed US occupations. Other
evidence finds rising demand for complementary work and no aggregate earnings or hours effect above
2% in Danish records after two years.

That is not a reason to dismiss the harm. Aggregate stability can coexist with a lost livelihood.
It is a reason to measure the change honestly and preserve the human accountability that does not
transfer to the model when a task is automated.

[Read the labour evidence and its limits.](ai-responsibility/06-work-livelihoods-and-accountability.md)

## What we are asking AI companies to do

1. **Publish enough provenance for one person to find their work.** A broad category is not an
   answer to an individual rights question.
2. **Establish authority before using data.** Record ownership, licence, consent, public-domain
   status or the legal exception relied upon. Being able to read something is not permission.
3. **Treat identity separately from file ownership.** A person should control realistic use of their
   face and voice, subject to legitimate public-interest and speech protections.
4. **Make user-data choices clear at first use.** State what is collected, retained, reviewed and
   used for training, with a real choice before the content is provided.
5. **Run staged releases proportionate to capability, reach and irreversibility.** Test first in a
   bounded environment, with independent access and predefined reasons to stop.
6. **Evaluate the system after adversarial modification.** For open weights, test what a capable
   downstream actor can make the model do, not only what the release build refuses.
7. **Remain accountable for every control point you retain.** The provider organisation, not one
  employee or downstream user, is accountable for its data, model design, safeguards, release,
  monitoring, incident response, withdrawal and the model-level consequences of those choices.
8. **Make failure visible.** Publish material incidents, limits, bypasses and corrective action with
   enough detail for independent scrutiny.
9. **Provide redress.** Give affected people a usable route to report, preserve evidence, remove
   harmful material, appeal and obtain remedy.
10. **Measure and share the economic outcome.** Report displacement as well as productivity, protect
    entry paths, support transitions and let the people whose work created the gain share in it.

None of these asks requires AI development to stop. They require evidence to grow with capability
and responsibility to grow with reach.

## What governments should make non-optional

- Enforce the provider organisation's accountability for the models it builds and releases. When a
  company does not uphold that responsibility voluntarily, policy and law must make it binding.
- A release gate for models whose capability, reach or irreversible distribution can create
  systemic harm.
- Independent evaluation and regulator access, not provider self-assessment alone.
- Training-data transparency, enforceable rights reservation and workable collective licensing or
  another remedy where individual negotiation cannot scale.
- A consistent right over digital replicas for ordinary people as well as public figures, with
  protection for legitimate reporting, criticism, history and art.
- Duties across the full chain: model provider, product provider, deployer, distributor and human
  decision-maker.
- Incident reporting, withdrawal powers and compensation that reaches the person harmed.
- Worker information, participation and transition obligations before AI changes livelihoods.

The exact mechanism is for democratic institutions to decide. The principle is: **the more
capable and widely deployed the system, the stronger and less optional its evidence and
accountability must become.**

## What enterprises and users still own

No provider safeguard releases the adopter from responsibility.

An enterprise should constrain access and action in the environment, name the human who can stop the
system, monitor what it actually did, preserve evidence and suspend it when the boundary fails.
That named human is accountable for the task or work they direct. Their accountability does not
absorb the provider organisation's responsibility for the model or its model-level consequences.

A human should not override an agent's refusal, remove the instruction, move to another tool or
perform the same violation manually. **It is your responsibility to make sure that neither the agent
breaks these rules, nor you do.**

## What Clover can do

Clover provides an agent with detailed rules in [`AGENTS.md`](../AGENTS.md). In one cycle, the agent can
make a boundary visible, explain the risk and refuse its own part before the act happens. Clover also
keeps the human accountable for the task or work they direct, including holding the same boundary and
monitoring the Outcome.

That is enforcement by the agent inside one cycle. At organisational, legal and industry scale,
Clover cannot enforce compliance.

Clover cannot bind the human, control a provider, withdraw a released model, compensate a harmed
person or enforce a rule across an industry. It does not pretend otherwise.

At model scale, the company building and releasing the model is accountable for that model and the
consequences of the controls it owns. At deployment scale, the deploying organisation is accountable
for how it gives the model access and authority. At task scale, the named human is accountable for
the work they direct. Governments must enforce the duties that companies and deployers do not uphold
voluntarily. **No layer may pass its responsibility downward and call the gap accountability.**

## Read and challenge the evidence

The complete evidence set begins at
[AI Responsibility: Evidence and the Ask](ai-responsibility/README.md). Every paper names what its
sources establish and what they do not.

No source reviewed establishes that AI companies share an intention to disrupt society or replace
people. The documented record is enough without assigning motive: warnings existed, safeguards have
limits, law lagged release, harms and benefits are uneven, and no one actor currently holds the whole
boundary.

If a claim is wrong, correction matters more than agreement.
[Open an issue](https://github.com/santhoshsathish94/clover-framework/issues) with the source that
changes it.
