# AI Responsibility: Evidence and the Ask

Status: evidence checked 6 September 2026.

This dossier asks why general-purpose AI reached global use before society had a binding way to
hold its builders, deployers and users responsible for what it could do.

The answer cannot be "nobody knew." Researchers, governments and companies named many of the
risks before ChatGPT, and leading institutions now publish detailed risk frameworks. The record
instead points to a gap between knowing and governing: the learning does not reliably control the
next release. Capability is measured and rewarded; frontier safety governance is often company-run;
governments promote and compete in AI while regulating it; and incidents do not automatically become
a shared, binding change.

The master argument is
[Why Responsibility for AI Was Sidelined](why-responsibility-was-sidelined.md). It brings the warning
record, the pursuit-of-dominance evidence, the causal conclusion and its limits into one document.

## Evidence standard

Each factual claim is linked to the source that supports it. Primary law, regulator findings,
company documentation and peer-reviewed work are preferred. Reporting is used when the underlying
record is unavailable or when the fact being established is what happened in public.

Each paper ends with an evidence ledger. Its final column matters: it says what the source **does
not** establish. A source can prove that a safeguard failed in one test without proving that all
safeguards always fail. A labour study can measure a decline in one market without proving an
aggregate global job loss. A proposed bill is not law.

Three labels keep the argument honest:

- **Observed:** directly supported by the cited record.
- **Conclusion:** Clover's interpretation of multiple observations.
- **Proposal:** what Clover asks responsible actors to do. It is a position, not a discovered fact.

## The conclusion in one page

1. The risks were discussed years before global adoption. The missing element was not awareness; it
   was binding authority over release.
2. Companies and governments do learn, but the learning is not sovereign. At OpenAI, leadership
   holds final release authority and a competitor release can trigger a guarded review of safety
   requirements. Governments pursue AI leadership while regulating it, and incidents do not
   automatically change the common floor.
3. General-purpose AI inherited software's deploy-first posture rather than medicine's staged,
   externally reviewed market gate. Calling a worldwide release a research preview did not make it
   a controlled trial.
4. Training at internet scale made individual permission, attribution and compensation hard, but
   not meaningless. Licences and contributor payments exist where parties chose to build them.
5. Law protects some works, uses, people and jurisdictions. It does not turn every harmful use into
   a legal violation. Legality is a floor, not the whole responsibility.
6. A model instruction can refuse. It cannot prove consent, bind a human, or prevent an open-weight
   copy from being modified. Enforcement has to exist in the product, environment, organisation and
   law as well as in the model.
7. Labour effects are already measurable in particular occupations and age groups, but the evidence
   does not show economy-wide replacement. That uncertainty calls for monitoring and transition
   duties, not for either panic or dismissal.

## The principle

**With more capability comes more responsibility. Responsibility cannot remain optional when the
capability affects people who never chose to participate.**

Clover cannot impose that rule on the world. It can state it clearly, give an agent rules for one
cycle, keep the evidence visible, and ask those with authority to make the boundary real.
