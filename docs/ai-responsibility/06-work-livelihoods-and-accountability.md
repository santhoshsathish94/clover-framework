# Work, Livelihoods and Accountability

Status: evidence checked 6 September 2026.

## The question

Has AI already destroyed livelihoods by being used to replace human work, and did organisations
account for the accountability they removed when they did it?

## The honest answer

**Observed:** There is measured displacement in particular online labour markets and an emerging
employment gap for young workers in AI-exposed occupations. Other large studies find no aggregate
loss in hours or earnings, and some markets show new demand alongside the decline.

**Not established:** No evidence reviewed for this dossier provides a reliable worldwide count of
jobs lost because of generative AI. Occupational exposure is not job loss. A company saying a layoff
is "because of AI" is not by itself a causal study.

**Conclusion:** The absence of proven economy-wide displacement does not erase concentrated harm.
People in a task, age group or market can lose work while aggregate employment stays flat. The right
response is measurement, worker participation, transition support and preserved human
accountability, not either a claim of universal destruction or a claim that nothing happened.

## Four measurements that must not be confused

### 1. Occupational exposure: what could change

The International Labour Organization and Poland's NASK analysed 29,753 occupational tasks with
52,558 human assessments, expert validation and AI-assisted scoring. Their
[2025 index](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure)
estimates that 25% of global employment is in occupations with some exposure to generative AI and
3.3% is in the highest exposure category.

The ILO explicitly says these figures are **potential exposure, not actual job losses**, and that job
transformation is more likely than full replacement. Exposure is a map of where to look, not a count
of people displaced.

### 2. Platform outcomes: what happened to particular freelancers

A peer-reviewed 2024 study by Xiang Hui, Oren Reshef and Luofeng Zhou examined freelancers on a large
online labour platform after ChatGPT, DALL-E 2 and Midjourney. The
[Organization Science paper](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2023.18441) found reductions in both
employment and earnings for workers in highly affected occupations. Past high performance did not
protect them; the authors found suggestive evidence that top freelancers were disproportionately
affected.

That is observed market harm, but it is one platform over a short period. It does not establish a
national or global employment effect.

A separate peer-reviewed study examined more than three million postings on a global freelance
platform from January 2021 to September 2023. The
[Oxford Internet Institute summary](https://www.oii.ox.ac.uk/news-events/the-winners-and-losers-of-generative-ai-in-the-freelance-job-market/)
reports 20-50% declines relative to the wider trend in selected substitutable writing and translation
tasks. It also reports that demand for chatbot and natural-language-processing work almost tripled
and that net freelance demand increased after ChatGPT's launch.

Both can be true. Aggregate demand can rise while a translator, writer or novice worker loses the
part of the market on which their livelihood depended.

### 3. Administrative records: what did not move at aggregate level

Anders Humlum and Emilie Vestergaard linked adoption surveys with Danish administrative labour
records. Their 2025 NBER working paper,
[Still Waters, Rapid Currents](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777), found widespread employer
initiatives, reported productivity gains and new AI-related tasks, but precise null effects on
recorded earnings and hours at worker and workplace level. The estimates rule out effects larger
than 2% two years after ChatGPT's launch.

This is strong counter-evidence to claims of immediate broad replacement. It is evidence from Denmark,
for the measured period and outcomes. It does not negate losses in a different market or prove that
longer-term effects will remain null.

### 4. Payroll patterns: an early warning, not a causal verdict

The Stanford Digital Economy Lab's
[August 2026 update](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publications/canaries-in-the-coal-mine/)
uses ADP payroll records covering millions of US workers through June 2026. It reports no widespread
economy-wide displacement, but says employment for workers aged 22-25 in AI-exposed occupations was
19% lower relative to the path of less-exposed peers. That is a relative gap, not a finding that 19%
of those workers lost jobs. The divergence came mainly through reduced hiring, not more separations,
and was concentrated where AI use substitutes for human tasks.

The authors explicitly call these **descriptive indicators rather than causal estimates**. They note
that some trends began before generative AI and that the gap is more pronounced in the ADP sample
than in national survey benchmarks. The result is a reason to investigate and protect entry paths,
not proof that AI alone caused a 19% loss.

## What can be said now

The evidence supports these statements:

- generative AI is changing the demand for particular tasks;
- losses and gains are distributed unevenly;
- writing, translation and some entry-level work show early adverse signals;
- complementary AI work has grown in some markets;
- aggregate employment, hours and earnings effects can remain small while concentrated groups are
  harmed;
- the outcome depends partly on organisational choices and public policy, not capability alone.

The evidence does not yet support these statements:

- AI has caused economy-wide mass unemployment;
- one global number describes jobs destroyed by AI;
- every exposed occupation will be automated;
- every productivity gain removes a job;
- the absence of a current aggregate effect means future effects will be harmless.

## The accountability loss is a separate problem

A job is a bundle of tasks, relationships, judgement and responsibility. Automating tasks does not
transfer all four to the model.

When an organisation removes a human role, several things can happen:

- the model performs some of the work;
- remaining workers absorb review and exception handling;
- a manager or vendor still decides whether to use the output;
- the affected customer or worker may not know who made the decision;
- responsibility can become less visible even when the work becomes cheaper.

The model cannot employ, dismiss, compensate or bargain with a worker. The deploying organisation
makes those choices. If the organisation removes the person who understood the work and does not name
who now checks and answers for the outcome, it has not automated accountability. It has left it
vacant.

The EU AI Act recognises part of this distinction. For high-risk workplace systems, Article 26
requires employers to inform affected workers and their representatives before use, assign human
oversight to people with competence and authority, monitor operation and suspend use when there is
reason to consider it risky. Those duties apply to defined high-risk uses, not every workplace tool,
but they show that deployment responsibility can be assigned to the employer rather than the model.

## Why capability-first adoption creates the wrong incentive

A capability demonstration answers: **can the model perform this task?**

A responsible adoption decision also asks:

- What part of the job is actually being changed?
- Who loses income, entry into the profession or bargaining power?
- Who checks the output and handles exceptions?
- Who remains named and answerable?
- What happens when the model is wrong?
- How will the gain be shared with the people whose work and data helped create it?

If the only measured Outcome is cost saved, every displaced responsibility appears as efficiency.
The missing costs move to workers, customers and the public because they are outside the measurement.

That is not a property of AI alone. It is a choice about what the adopting organisation counts.

---

## What responsible adoption would require

This is Clover's proposal.

1. **Measure before adoption.** Establish the current headcount, workload, quality, error, customer
   outcome and entry-level hiring baseline before introducing the tool.
2. **Tell workers before the decision.** Explain what is being tested, what data it uses, which tasks
   may change, how performance will be judged and who can stop the deployment.
3. **Run a bounded comparison.** Compare AI-assisted work, existing work and any proposed automated
   path on real outcomes, including correction labour and downstream harm.
4. **Preserve a named accountable human.** Do not remove the role that understands and answers for
   the work while claiming the model replaced it.
5. **Separate augmentation from replacement.** Record which tasks became easier, which positions were
   removed, which were created and which work moved invisibly to someone else.
6. **Protect entry paths.** Monitor junior hiring and training. A profession that automates its first
   rung may later discover it has no experienced people.
7. **Share the gain.** Use productivity gains for better work, reduced drudgery, transition support
   and broad benefit, not only headcount reduction.
8. **Publish the outcome.** Report actual employment, earnings, quality and incident effects after
   deployment. Do not present a forecast or executive claim as observed success.
