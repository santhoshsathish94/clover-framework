# Why Incidents Still Do Not Become Accountability

Status: evidence checked 6 September 2026.

## The question

If one person can see the need for responsibility and build a framework such as Clover around it,
why cannot governments, policymakers and the companies building AI do the same?

Do they not know how? Do they refuse to learn? Why are similar failures still allowed after repeated
incidents while model capability continues to grow?

## The short answer

**Observed:** Governments and leading AI companies know many of the mechanisms. They publish risk
frameworks, evaluations, principles, incident procedures and proposed controls. Some have revised
those frameworks after experience. The knowledge is incomplete, but absence of all know-how is not a
credible explanation.

**Conclusion:** The missing part is not another principle. It is a feedback loop with enough authority
to make an incident change the next release.

Today, capability has a strong scoreboard, immediate rewards and concentrated decision authority.
Responsibility is divided among providers, deployers, platforms, users and regulators; much of it is
self-assessed; and the cost of restraint is treated as lost innovation or competitive position. An
incident may change one filter or one policy without changing the system that produced it.

There is no evidence of one shared intention to cause harm or refuse learning. The evidence supports
a structural failure instead:

> The institutions are capable of learning, but the learning is not sovereign. It does not reliably
> outrank release, revenue, adoption or national competition.

In Clover's terms, the world records Outcomes but often does not complete Growth.

---

## They know enough to act

The leading developers do not operate without safety concepts.

OpenAI's
[2025 Preparedness Framework](https://openai.com/index/updating-our-preparedness-framework/)
defines capability thresholds, evaluations, safeguards reports and deployment conditions for severe
risks. Anthropic's
[Responsible Scaling Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/responsible-scaling-policy) defines
capability thresholds, required safeguards, risk reports and conditions under which scaling may need
to pause. Google DeepMind's
[Frontier Safety Framework](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/strengthening-our-frontier-safety-framework/)
uses critical capability levels, early-warning evaluations and safety cases before relevant launches.

Governments have named the same governance requirements. The UK's 2023
[pro-innovation AI white paper](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-regulation-a-pro-innovation-approach/white-paper)
identified safety, transparency, fairness, accountability and governance, and contestability and
redress. The EU AI Act now assigns binding duties for defined high-risk systems and general-purpose
models with systemic risk.

These documents do not prove that every risk is understood or every mechanism works. They do disprove
the simple answer that the relevant institutions have never thought about responsibility or do not
know any way to implement it.

---

## Capability has the stronger scoreboard

The clearest measured asymmetry is not in what organisations say. It is in what they consistently
measure and disclose.

The [2026 Stanford AI Index](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/responsible-ai)
reports that almost all leading frontier developers publish capability results on benchmarks such as
MMLU and SWE-bench, while reporting on responsible-AI benchmarks remains sparse. It records 362 AI
incidents in 2025, up from 233 in 2024. It also reports that average scores in the Foundation Model
Transparency Index rose from 37 in 2023 to 58 in 2024, then fell to 40 in 2025, with major gaps in
training-data, compute and post-deployment-impact disclosure.

At the same time, the Index reports that:

- [industry produced more than 90% of notable models](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/research-and-development)
  in 2025;
- [frontier capability saturated difficult benchmarks rapidly](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/technical-performance);
- [corporate AI investment more than doubled](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/economy),
  and organisational adoption reached 88%.

These measures do not prove a company's motive. They show that capability, capital and adoption have
shared, comparable and frequently updated measures, while responsibility remains less consistently
measured and less transparent.

A common scoreboard makes capability easy to race. Sparse responsibility reporting leaves less
shared evidence for comparing whether safeguards improve.

## The adviser and the final decision-maker are often inside the same organisation

Internal safety governance can be serious and still contain a structural conflict.

OpenAI says its cross-functional internal Safety Advisory Group reviews whether safeguards
sufficiently minimise severe risk and recommends responses ranging from approval to further testing
or stronger protection. The same document says those recommendations go to **OpenAI Leadership for
the final decision**.

Anthropic's policy contains board approval, external review mechanisms, internal reporting and
published risk reports. It also remains a policy written and revised by Anthropic. Google DeepMind
performs safety-case reviews through its own Frontier Safety Framework.

There is nothing inherently wrong with an organisation governing its own work. Every organisation
must do that. The gap appears when internal governance is the final release gate for a product whose
failure can affect people outside the organisation.

The decisive question is not whether a company has a safety team. It is:

> Can the safety finding stop the release without permission from the same organisation that wants
to release it?

Where the answer is no, responsibility advises power rather than constrains it.

## Competition makes a safety floor negotiable

The race is not only rhetoric imposed by critics. It appears in first-party policy.

OpenAI's Preparedness Framework says that if another frontier developer releases a high-risk system
without comparable safeguards, OpenAI may adjust its own requirements. It says it would first
rigorously confirm that the risk landscape changed, publicly acknowledge the adjustment, assess that
it does not meaningfully increase the overall risk of severe harm, and keep safeguards at a more
protective level. Those conditions matter. So does the governance fact that a competitor's release
can change the company's safety requirements.

Governments reinforce the same pressure.

The 2025 US
[AI Action Plan](https://www.whitehouse.gov/articles/2025/07/white-house-unveils-americas-ai-action-plan/)
is titled *Winning the AI Race*. It calls for removing federal regulations that hinder AI development
and deployment, rapid infrastructure buildout and exporting US AI. The White House release says the
plan will “cement U.S. dominance in artificial intelligence” and quotes AI and Crypto Czar David
Sacks saying that, to remain the leading economic and military power, the United States must win the
AI race.

That competitive posture did not begin with AI. A 2024
[White House trade fact sheet](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefing-room/statements-releases/2024/05/14/fact-sheet-president-biden-takes-action-to-protect-american-workers-and-businesses-from-chinas-unfair-trade-practices/)
treated electric vehicles, batteries and solar cells as strategic sectors requiring protection from
Chinese industrial power. It reported that China's EV exports grew 70% from 2022 to 2023, that China
controlled over 80% of certain EV-battery supply-chain segments, and that it dominated 80-90% of
certain parts of the global solar supply chain. The US response included higher tariffs and domestic
investment intended to protect and rebuild American capacity.

The 2024 trade policy and 2025 AI plan do not prove that China's clean-technology position caused the
later AI policy. Together they establish a wider pattern in which technological leadership is treated
as economic, national-security and geopolitical power. Clover's interpretation is that AI became a
field the United States was unwilling to lose after China had already secured leading positions in
other strategic industries.

The EU's
[AI Continent Action Plan](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/ai-continent-action-plan)
combines its binding AI Act with tens of billions of euros for AI factories and gigafactories,
accelerated adoption, more data-centre capacity, simplified rules and an explicit goal of global AI
leadership.

This does not mean safety commitments are false. It means the state is not only a neutral regulator.
It is also an investor, purchaser, promoter and geopolitical competitor. A government asked to slow
a domestic company may believe it is also weakening national security, economic growth or influence.

When every actor fears being the only one to pause, voluntary restraint is unstable. A common,
non-negotiable floor is the mechanism that prevents responsibility from becoming a competitive
disadvantage.

## Governments have made deliberate choices, not merely technical mistakes

The UK's 2023 white paper states the trade-off unusually plainly.

The paper retains accountability and governance, and contestability and redress, among its five
principles. It also says a pro-innovation approach involves **tolerating a certain degree of risk
rather than intervening in all cases**. It initially kept those cross-sector principles
non-statutory, chose not to create a new AI regulator, and said it was too soon to change life-cycle
accountability and liability. It also acknowledged that:

- AI risks fall between regulatory remits;
- some regulators have limited capacity and AI expertise;
- responsibility across opaque AI supply chains is difficult to allocate;
- assigning too much responsibility to foundation-model developers could hamper innovation;
- the initial framework created no new rights or routes to redress.

That is not evidence of secret wrongdoing. It is evidence that policymakers saw the accountability
gap and initially chose gradual, flexible intervention while explicitly prioritising proportionate
burdens and preserving innovation.

The EU AI Act proves that binding duties are possible. As of 6 September 2026, its obligations and
enforcement powers apply to general-purpose AI models: providers of models with systemic risk must
evaluate them, assess and mitigate risk, report serious incidents and give regulators access, while
the Commission can impose fines and request restriction, withdrawal or recall. High-risk-system
duties remain phased: rules for systems listed in Annex III are due from 2 December 2027, and rules
for high-risk systems embedded in regulated products from 2 August 2028. When those duties apply,
most stand-alone high-risk systems use provider internal control rather than mandatory third-party
assessment.

Government action is therefore neither absent nor complete. It is late, scoped and shaped by the
same innovation trade-off.

## Fragmented responsibility can become no responsibility

An AI outcome can pass through several actors:

- a model provider trains and releases the capability;
- a product provider builds the interface and safeguards;
- an enterprise gives it data, tools and authority;
- a platform distributes the output;
- a human directs, approves or republishes it;
- several regulators each control one legal issue.

After harm, each actor can point to a real limit in its control:

- the provider says the user misused a general-purpose tool;
- the deployer says the model failed unexpectedly;
- the platform says it did not create the material;
- the user says the model produced it;
- one regulator says the conduct belongs to another remit or jurisdiction.

Each statement can be partly true. Together they can leave the harmed person without one actor
answerable for the complete outcome.

The EU AI Act attempts to assign duties along the value chain. Globally, however, there is no single
rule that closes every handoff. Distributed participation is necessary. Distributed accountability
without a named owner is abandonment.

## An incident is not Growth

A list of incidents is Context. A postmortem is learning. Neither is Growth on its own.

An incident becomes Growth only when all of the following happen:

1. **It is visible.** The event is reported through a common process rather than discovered only by a
   victim, journalist or lawsuit.
2. **The accountable organisations own it.** The provider organisation owns model-level failures,
   the deploying organisation owns its deployment, and the public authority enforces the duties that
   apply. Named humans remain answerable for the tasks and decisions they direct inside those layers.
3. **The cause is established.** Investigation reaches the model, data, product, deployment and human
   decision instead of stopping at "user misuse" or "AI error."
4. **The system changes.** The unsafe capability, access, process or incentive is corrected.
5. **The correction is tested.** Evidence shows the change holds under the conditions that produced
   the failure.
6. **The lesson travels.** A material incident changes the minimum rule for later releases and other
   actors exposed to the same failure mode.

Current mechanisms rarely guarantee all six.

The OECD's AI Incidents and Hazards Monitor is explicitly a beta media-discourse monitor. It improves
shared visibility, but it is not a regulator or a universal investigation and recall system. The EU
AI Act now creates mandatory reporting for defined high-risk systems and systemic-risk general-purpose
models. Outside such regimes and scopes, reporting and correction remain uneven.

This is why similar mistakes recur. The incident reaches the public, but its lesson does not
necessarily reach the decision rule for the next system.

## Are they unwilling to learn?

A claim that governments and companies simply do not want to learn would go beyond the evidence.
There is evidence of real learning:

- Anthropic publishes multiple revisions of its Responsible Scaling Policy and disclosed a small
   number of timing and evaluation-method instances where it fell short of the full letter of its
   requirements, which it assessed as posing minimal risk;
- OpenAI says its framework incorporates testing, external expertise and field lessons;
- Google DeepMind has repeatedly expanded its risk domains and assessment process;
- the EU moved from non-binding ethics principles to enforceable law.

But learning is not the same as accepting accountability.

An organisation can learn how to patch one jailbreak while retaining the same release authority. A
government can learn that liability is unclear while postponing its allocation. A company can improve
safety while making its minimum depend partly on competitors. A policymaker can record an incident
without giving it power to interrupt deployment.

So the strongest supported answer is:

> They are learning, but they have not made learning binding. Capability is allowed to continue by
> default; responsibility must repeatedly win an internal, political or legal argument.

That default is the real decision.

## What would make accountability real

This is Clover's proposal, not a description of current global law.

### 1. Give incidents a mandatory path

Create a common incident taxonomy and reporting duty for frontier and widely deployed general-purpose
systems, not only selected high-risk applications. Preserve evidence, notify affected people and
publish appropriately redacted findings.

### 2. Make serious incidents trigger a decision

Define thresholds that automatically require independent investigation, temporary capability or
access limits, and renewed approval. Do not let the provider decide whether the event is important
enough to reopen release.

### 3. Name the accountable organisation at every layer

For each model and deployment, publicly identify the accountable provider organisation, deploying
organisation and public authority. Named humans should own concrete tasks and decisions inside each
organisation, but an organisation must not move model-level accountability onto one employee, a
contractor or the people using the model. Contracts may divide work; they should not erase
responsibility to the person affected.

### 4. Separate advice from approval

Internal safety teams should continue to advise. For capability and reach above defined thresholds,
an independent authority should decide whether the evidence supports release. Its budget and mandate
must not depend on the release succeeding.

### 5. Make the safety floor independent of competitors

A competitor's unsafe release can increase the danger in the world. It should not lower another
provider's minimum safeguards. Common rules exist precisely because unilateral restraint is unstable.

### 6. Make the correction travel

When an investigation confirms a material failure mode, every provider and deployer in scope should
evaluate it, report whether they are exposed, and demonstrate the mitigation. Aviation and medicine
do not treat every repeated failure as an unrelated company's private lesson.

### 7. Measure responsibility beside capability

Every frontier release should publish comparable results for safety, privacy, fairness, security,
post-deployment impact and unresolved risk alongside capability benchmarks. Missing evidence should
be visible as missing, not converted into an assumption of safety.

### 8. Separate the state's conflicting roles

The body promoting AI investment and adoption should not be the only body judging whether a system is
safe enough. Regulation needs independent authority, technical capacity and funding equal to the
scale of the industry it oversees.

### 9. Give harmed people standing and redress

A feedback loop that hears only providers is not learning about societal Outcome. People affected by
an AI system need a route to explanation, evidence preservation, appeal, removal and compensation.

---

## The direct answer

Governments and AI companies do know many of the steps. The reason the problem remains is not one
missing framework and not one hidden motive.

It remains because:

- those who gain from release often retain the final decision;
- capability and adoption are measured and rewarded faster than prevented harm;
- competition makes unilateral restraint costly;
- governments pursue leadership and investment while also regulating;
- legal authority is fragmented by use, sector and border;
- incidents do not automatically trigger independent review or a shared rule change;
- responsibility is divided across the chain until no one owns the full Outcome.

Clover does not solve that institutional problem. It makes the missing step visible:

> **Outcome without binding Growth is how a system repeats a mistake while continuing to call itself
> responsible.**

The question for every company and government is therefore no longer "Do you have responsible-AI
principles?" It is:

> **What verified incident can stop your next release, who has authority to stop it, and what rule
> will change for everyone after it happens?**

If those questions have no public answer, accountability has not yet been built.
