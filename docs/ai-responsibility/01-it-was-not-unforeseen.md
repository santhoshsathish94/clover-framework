# It Was Not Unforeseen

Status: evidence checked 6 September 2026.

## The question

When an individual can ask why AI was released before its effects on consent, privacy, work,
misinformation and accountability were understood, what happened to the researchers and governments
that should have asked first?

## The answer

**Observed:** They did ask. The record contains repeated warnings and principles before ChatGPT
reached the public.

**Conclusion:** Awareness was not converted into a binding release gate. The actors who could name a
risk were not necessarily the actors with authority to stop deployment, and the instruments adopted
by governments were initially principles and voluntary frameworks rather than pre-market approval.

That conclusion is different from saying every risk was known, every warning was correct, or every
company ignored safety. The record supports none of those absolutes.

---

## 2017: the industry named responsibility and race pressure

The [Asilomar AI Principles](https://futureoflife.org/open-letter/ai-principles/) were developed at
the Beneficial AI conference and published in 2017. The current record lists 1,797 AI and robotics
researchers among 5,720 signatories, including senior figures associated at the time with DeepMind,
OpenAI, Facebook, Google and major universities.

The principles did not speak only about distant catastrophic risk. They said:

- teams should cooperate to avoid corner-cutting on safety standards;
- systems should be safe throughout their operational lifetime and verifiably so where feasible;
- designers and builders are stakeholders in the moral implications of use and misuse;
- people should control the data they generate;
- prosperity created by AI should be shared broadly;
- humans should choose whether and how to delegate decisions to AI.

Those are close to the questions now being asked about model release, data, work and accountability.
The principles were a public commitment. They were not a regulator, a licence or a power to stop a
release.

## 2019 and 2021: governments adopted the principles

In May 2019, governments adopted the
[OECD AI Principles](https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/sub-issues/ai-principles.html), described by the
OECD as the first intergovernmental AI standard. Their five values include human rights and privacy,
transparency, safety and accountability. Their policy recommendations include preparing for labour
market transformation.

In November 2021, all 193 UNESCO member states adopted the
[Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence](https://www.unesco.org/en/artificial-intelligence/recommendation-ethics).
It calls for risk assessment, privacy through the AI lifecycle, auditability, impact assessment,
human oversight and a rule that AI must not displace ultimate human responsibility.

These records disprove the claim that governments had not encountered the questions. They do not
show that governments had created a common enforcement body, a pre-market approval process or a
uniform liability rule. A recommendation can establish agreement and guide national policy. It
cannot refuse market access by itself.

## 2020-2021: researchers warned about scale and undocumented data

Emily Bender, Timnit Gebru, Angelina McMillan-Major and Margaret Mitchell asked the direct question in
[On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots: Can Language Models Be Too Big?](https://doi.org/10.1145/3442188.3445922).
The peer-reviewed paper recommends weighing environmental and financial cost, curating and documenting
data instead of ingesting everything on the web, and evaluating stakeholder values before
development.

The employment dispute around that paper matters, but it must be stated carefully. MIT Technology
Review reported that Google said the draft did not meet its publication bar, while Gebru said the
company forced her out and cut off her access before a negotiated departure. The precise sequence was
contested. The documented fact is that a co-lead of Google's Ethical AI team left Google in December
2020 during a dispute over a paper warning about the company's core model direction. The
[contemporaneous account](https://www.technologyreview.com/2020/12/04/1013294/google-ai-ethics-research-paper-forced-out-timnit-gebru/)
also records that more than 1,400 Google staff and 1,900 other supporters signed a protest letter.

In February 2021, Google fired Margaret Mitchell, the founder and other co-head of the same Ethical
AI team. The [BBC's contemporaneous report](https://www.bbc.com/news/technology-56135817) says Google
claimed an investigation found that she had moved files outside the company in violation of its code
of conduct. It also reports that Mitchell had been locked out of Google's systems for five weeks,
had criticised Gebru's departure, and that both women had raised concerns about censorship inside
the company.

This proves neither a conspiracy nor that Google rejected every recommendation. It shows that the
warning existed inside a leading developer, that the researchers asking the questions did not carry
final release authority, and that dissent around the work carried professional consequences. The
available record does not prove that competitive dominance was the sole motive for either employment
decision.

**Clover's conclusion:** Responsibility was sidelined at the system level because capability,
commercial direction and competitive dominance held decision power while ethics warnings remained
advisory. That is an interpretation of the pattern, not a claim that one hidden motive explains every
individual action.

## 2022: known limitations went into a worldwide research preview

OpenAI's [30 November 2022 launch post](https://openai.com/index/chatgpt/) called ChatGPT a free
"research preview" intended to collect user feedback. The same post disclosed that the model could
produce plausible but false answers, respond differently to rephrased prompts, guess instead of ask,
and sometimes answer harmful requests. It said the external content filter had false negatives and
false positives.

That is meaningful disclosure. It is also evidence that known limitations were tested through public
deployment. Reuters reported, citing UBS and Similarweb, that ChatGPT reached an estimated
[100 million monthly active users by January 2023](https://www.reuters.com/technology/chatgpt-sets-record-fastest-growing-user-base-analyst-note-2023-02-01/),
two months after launch.

A public preview can teach a developer how a system behaves in the world. It also makes the public
part of the learning environment before an external authority has decided whether the experiment is
ready for that reach.

## 2023: a pause was requested and did not become a gate

The [Pause Giant AI Experiments letter](https://futureoflife.org/open-letter/pause-giant-ai-experiments/)
called for a public, verifiable six-month pause on training systems more powerful than GPT-4. Its
current page records 33,705 signatures. It requested shared safety protocols overseen by independent
experts, regulatory authorities, provenance, auditing, liability for AI-caused harm and institutions
to manage economic disruption.

It did not create any authority capable of ordering a pause. Development continued. The Future of
Life Institute's own
[one-year review](https://futureoflife.org/ai/the-pause-letter-one-year-later/) says companies
accelerated investment instead. That review is evidence of the signatory organisation's assessment,
not independent proof of any company's motive.

Also in January 2023, NIST released the
[AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework). NIST states
that it is intended for **voluntary use**. It gives organisations a serious way to manage risk; it
is not permission that must be obtained before release.

---

## 2024-2027: binding law began after mass adoption

The EU AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024. Its general-purpose-model obligations began in
August 2025, its transparency rules began in August 2026, and many high-risk-system obligations apply
from 2027. The Act now provides real enforcement: regulator access, fines, withdrawal powers,
pre-market conformity assessment for defined high-risk uses and obligations for systemic-risk
models. It is not merely another principle.

The chronology still matters. ChatGPT had reached an estimated 100 million monthly users in January
2023. The first comprehensive binding regime arrived later and applies different duties according to
risk and use. It did not act as an approval gate before the original global release.

## Why warning did not become control

The evidence supports a structural explanation, not a claim about one hidden motive:

1. **The early instruments had no stop authority.** Asilomar was an open letter; OECD and UNESCO were
   principles and recommendations; NIST explicitly made its framework voluntary.
2. **Release authority remained with the developer.** Internal researchers could advise or dissent,
   but their organisations controlled the product decision.
3. **The product could scale faster than law.** A free digital service reached an estimated 100
   million monthly users in two months; legislation took years to negotiate and phase in.
4. **Responsibility was divided.** The model provider, application provider, deploying organisation,
   distribution platform and end user each control different parts. Without a rule assigning the
   whole chain, each actor can satisfy its own narrow duty while the outcome still harms someone.
5. **Public deployment was treated as part of development.** "Research preview" and "iterative
   deployment" made real-world use a source of feedback. That can improve a system, but it reverses
   the ordinary logic of a controlled trial: broad exposure happens before the learning is complete.

## Clover's conclusion

The failure was not a shortage of ethics language. It was the absence of a boundary that the actor
with release authority could not waive.

A warning without authority is Context. It is not enforcement.
