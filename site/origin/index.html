Origin

Twelve discoveries from 1943 to 2026 — one simple story and visual diagram each.

Behind every milestone in AI is a human story: a researcher who hit a real wall, saw why the old idea failed, and built a simple mechanism to get past it. We built and ran all eleven foundational papers (1943–2017) from scratch, and put them to work inside Clover AI Studio.

Chapter 1 of 12 · 1943 · Warren S. McCulloch & Walter Pitts

A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity

A brain cell firing or staying silent is a binary logic gate.

Hold a cold metal rod against your skin and it feels cold. Tap it for a split second and pull it away, and you feel a flash of burning heat. McCulloch and nineteen-year-old Walter Pitts showed why using only timing and logic: when cold stops suddenly, a delayed two-tick relay reaches the heat nerve right after the cold veto turns off. By treating neurons as threshold gates and circular loops as memory, they proved any logical rule can be wired from simple cells.

In Clover: Inhibitory vetoes block out-of-domain memory and tool calls before generation begins.

Chapter 2 of 12 · 1950 · Alan M. Turing

Computing Machinery and Intelligence

One stored-program machine can become any machine — and learn from words instead of blows.

In Turing’s Imitation Game, the judge asks: “Add 34957 to 70764.” The witness pauses for 30 seconds and answers 105621 — forgetting to carry the 1 into the hundreds column (105721). Turing planted that slip on purpose: raw speed gives a machine away immediately. True intelligence comes from storing instructions as numbers in memory so a “Child-Machine” can learn from symbolic language rather than 22,400 blind punishment blows.

In Clover: Personal Process Learning (PPL) learns a new habit from one natural sentence instead of retraining weights.

Chapter 3 of 12 · 1956 · Allen Newell & Herbert A. Simon

The Logic Theory Machine

A quick three-number glance filters out 93.6% of dead ends before hard work begins.

Before their computer was ready, Newell and Simon tested the world’s first AI program by handing index cards to family members and students in a living room. To avoid drowning in millions of combinations, they gave the program a fast 3-number structural check (CSm: left depth, right depth, variable count) that skips 93.6% of dead ends instantly — and proved 48 of 67 theorems when prior proofs were kept in memory.

In Clover: Structural CSm signatures with the *2.07 contraction fix prune 2-hop ontology search steps by 22.5×.

Chapter 4 of 12 · 1958 · Frank Rosenblatt

The Perceptron

A zero-sum weight rule keeps a rare class from being drowned out by a common one.

Rosenblatt modeled the visual cortex as a random web of nerve fibers (S → A → R) that learns by turning up active weights. To stop a 3-to-1 majority class from shouting over a rarer class, his zero-sum γ-rule forces active fibers to steal weight from silent fibers so total pool weight stays zero. Whenever same-class overlap beats cross-class overlap (P_c11 > P_a > P_c12), accuracy reaches 100%.

In Clover: Zero-sum γ-balancing keeps large memory folders from drowning out smaller folders during recall.

Chapter 5 of 12 · 1969 · Marvin Minsky & Seymour Papert

Perceptrons: An Introduction to Computational Geometry

Local pinhole observers can count pixels, but cannot tell if a maze is connected.

Minsky and Papert proved that observers peeking through tiny 2-pixel or 3-pixel pinholes can never tell whether a spiral is one connected path or two separate paths, or whether a set of boxes all contain at least one dot (One-in-a-Box). Connectivity and conjunctive presence require matching across every required box or tracing with a serial pointer.

In Clover: Order-k One-in-a-Box checks require both a domain anchor and a mechanism match before injecting ontology records (cutting false positives from 16/16 to 0/16).

Chapter 6 of 12 · 1982 · John J. Hopfield

Collective Associative Memory

Symmetric two-way weights turn stored memories into valleys that pull noisy clues downhill.

Human memory retrieves a whole song from three notes without knowing a memory address. Hopfield showed that when synapses pull equally in both directions (T_ij = T_ji) and neurons flip one at a time, energy never rises (0 uphill steps in 7,162 flips) and corrupted cues roll straight into the stored memory valley.

In Clover: Bipolar {−1, +1} associative cleanup reconstructs related memory constellations from partial query clues.

Chapter 7 of 12 · 1986 · David E. Rumelhart, Geoffrey E. Hinton & Ronald J. Williams

Learning Representations by Back-Propagating Errors

Smooth S-curves let error signals flow backward so hidden layers invent their own features.

Hard 0/1 switches have zero slope, leaving middle neurons blind to how their weights affect the output. Replacing hard switches with smooth sigmoids let errors whisper backward through deep layers: 4 hidden neurons invented a thermometer abacus to solve Parity, and 2 hidden neurons solved 6-bit Mirror Symmetry with a 1 : 2 : 4 balance beam.

In Clover: Non-saturating {0.1, 0.9} targets keep compilation survival gradients alive when rolling back broken code statements.

Chapter 8 of 12 · 1988 · Richard S. Sutton

Learning to Predict by Temporal Differences

Updating today’s prediction toward tomorrow’s step wipes out lucky fluke noise.

Suppose a “Bad” board position loses 90% of the time. You try a “Novel” opening move that lands straight in “Bad,” and then win by a 1-in-10 miracle roll. A supervised learner pairs Novel → Win and calls it a 100% move. Sutton’s TD(0) updates Novel toward the next state (Bad = 10%), converging to the exact maximum-likelihood Markov model.

In Clover: Backward TD(0) pinpoints the exact statement where generated Python or Rust code breaks (6/6 localized).

Chapter 9 of 12 · 1998 · Yann LeCun, Léon Bottou, Yoshua Bengio & Patrick Haffner

Gradient-Based Learning Applied to Document Recognition

Shared convolutional weights and graph path scoring read whole check amounts without hand-cut boxes.

Cutting touching handwritten digits apart before reading them turns the right half of a 4 into a 1. LeNet-5 (60,000 shared weights driving 340,908 connections) and Graph Transformer Networks keep every candidate cut in a directed graph and score all valid paths together with Discriminative Forward Loss (logadd).

In Clover: GTN Forward scoring (logadd) combines parallel corroborating provenance paths and promotes active corrections over superseded ones.

Chapter 10 of 12 · 2012 · Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever & Geoffrey E. Hinton

ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks

Non-saturating ReLU, local lateral competition (LRN), and two GPUs ignited deep learning at scale.

Splitting a 60.97M-parameter network across two GTX 580 cards caused GPU 1 to specialize in black-and-white edges and GPU 2 in color blobs. Swapping saturating tanh for a simple one-way valve (ReLU(x) = max(0, x)) trained 7.8× faster and cut ImageNet error from 26.2% to 15.3%.

In Clover: Cross-channel LRN suppresses redundant memory hits so distinct facets surface together.

Chapter 11 of 12 · 2017 · Ashish Vaswani et al.

Attention Is All You Need

Replacing sequential recurrence with an open round table (O(1) Self-Attention) and parallel spotlights created the Transformer.

Instead of passing messages word-by-word down a bucket brigade (O(n)), Self-Attention seats every token around one table so any word can attend to any other in one step (O(1)). Scaling dot products by 1/√d_k prevents softmax saturation, while Multi-Head subspaces let each head focus on a distinct relationship (782× lower subspace MSE).

In Clover: Orthogonal multi-head subspaces (h = 3) unmix concept principles, empirical measurements, and active corrections.

Chapter 12 of 12 · 2026 · Clover AI Studio

Semantics at the Center. Core and Supporting Layers Around It.

A raw model alone solves 47.1% of our 70-task suite. Surrounding it with Clover’s Semantics Center, 3 Core Layers, and 3 Supporting Layers takes the exact same model to 100.0%.

Seventy-four years of invention (1943–2017) built extraordinary neural engines, yet a bare model still guesses arithmetic, hallucinates private records, and repeats corrected mistakes. Clover AI Studio places Semantics (CSI + Personal Process Learning) at the center to set meaning and direction first, drives execution through 3 Core Layers (C1 Inference, C2 Plan, C3 Decode), and grounds every turn in 3 Supporting Layers (S1 Memory, S2 Relationships, S3 Tools).

Result: All three switchable models (kimi-k3, nemotron-3.5-super-vl, lfm3-1.7b) reach 100% (70/70) on the 70-task suite and 100% (11/11) on live Scenario & Personal Process Learning.
